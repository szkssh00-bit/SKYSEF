Concept

Science communication for the next generation

SKYSEF is an international forum where junior and senior high school students discuss sustainable society and environment in the 21st century. Students share research in science, technology, mathematics and ICT, then consider what actions can be taken based on their findings.

Since its launch in 2012, SKYSEF has connected schools that actively promote inquiry-based research. The forum aims to develop scientific literacy, independent learning, and the ability to discuss scientific ideas in English with peers from different countries and regions.

Abstract:
This study investigates the "wet-darkening effect" and analyzes the impact of varying wetness levels and liquid refractive indices on optical properties. Our experiments first demonstrated that the "Total Internal Reflection (Venue:TIR) theory" provides insufficient explanation for fabric media, leading the research to focus on the "Forward Scattering Enhancement theory." Using a self-developed optical measurement device and "Relative Gain Analysis," we quantified variables into three key components: the Filling Factor, the Refractive Index Matching Factor, and the Attenuation Correction Factor. The results confirm that liquid filling reduces interfacial reflection and enhances light penetration through refractive index matching. This constricts the forward scattering path and reduces backward diffuse reflection, ultimately resulting in the observed darkening of visual brightness.
Abstract:
Normal solar cells only make electricity when there is light, so they always need extra batteries to store energy. To fix this problem, this study introduces a new hybrid dye-sensitized solar cell (Venue:HDSSC) that can produce power even in the dark. The researchers put a liquid iodine electrolyte into a cellulose filter paper. Under light conditions, it works as a normal solar cell. In the dark, it acts as both a chemical concentration cell and an electric double-layer capacitor. The tiny spaces in the cellulose fibers absorb the electrolyte. This creates a difference in ion density between the inside and outside of the cellulose, making chemical power. At the same time, ions gather at the electrodes to store and release electrical energy like a capacitor. While the normal liquid electrolyte showed zero voltage in the dark, this hybrid solar cell successfully maintained a high voltage of 591±45 mV.

Effects of Concentration, Temperature, and Salt Bridge on Electrical Output In this study, we investigated the factors influencing the electrical output of a concentration cell, including electrode materials (Venue:Cu, Zn, Al), concentration gradient, temperature, and the presence of a salt bridge. The objective was to identify conditions that enhance voltage and current generation. Each variable was systematically tested under controlled experimental conditions. Abstract:
Paramecium bursaria exhibits taxis, a behavior in which it alters its movement in response to external stimuli. The collective behavior of paramecium bursaria induced by taxis can be divided into two categories: physical factors, in which the organism is passively drawn toward a stimulus; and biological factors, in which the organism perceives the stimulus and actively modifies its own behavior. We thought that using a physical model might allow us to distinguish between these two factors. We adopted statistical mechanics as the model. If we regard Paramecium bursaria as physical particles, we can classify movements that follow statistical mechanics as those induced by physical factors, and those that contradict it as those induced by biological factors. We aim to use this approach to elucidate the properties of each type of taxis. In this study, we focused on electrochemotaxis and phototaxis.

Abstract:
Stag beetles are highly popular insects as pets in the world. During the larval stage, a pair of orange spot is observed exclusively on the abdomens of female larvae. These structures commonly referred to as “female spot”, have long been considered as ovary primordia and widely used for sex identification during larval period. However, no anatomical evidence has been presented to support this assumption. In this study, we tested the hypothesis that female spots are not ovary primordia but instead function as organs harboring symbiotic microorganisms. First, we examined the presence and size of female spot in both sexes and found that some male larvae also possessed these structures. Second, Analysis based on paraffin sections and SEM revealed that the female spots are matrix-like structure on the digestive tract, indicating that they are unlikely to be ovary primordia.Third, we identified microorganisms within the female spots and the mycangia by sequencing PCR-amplified 26S rRNA genes. The detected microorganisms were xylose-fermenting yeasts, which were the same species as those detected from the mycangia of adult females. Overturning the conventional view documented in classification guides, our results strongly suggest that female spots are not ovary primordia but symbiotic organs that harbor symbiotic yeasts. Our findings call for a revision of the conventional interpretation of female spots and provide new insights into symbiosis in stag beetles.

Abstract:
Honeybees share food with each other through a behavior called trophallaxis, in which a recipient extends its proboscis to drink nectar from a donor. Understanding how this behavior is initiated is key to revealing communication mechanisms in social insects. Recipients frequently tap the donor’s mandibles with their antennae; however, the functional role of antennal movements (Venue:AMs) remains unclear due to their rapid, complex motion. Here, we show that specific AMs in recipients play a causal role in trophallaxis using an AI-based pose-tracking tool (Venue:DeepLabCut) in combination with behavioral assays. By tracking interactions between harnessed bees, we found that recipient antennae were oriented toward donors before proboscis extension and exhibited high-frequency (Venue:>50 Hz) vibrations during trophallaxis, whereas no specific AMs were observed in donors. To test causality, we compared donor responses when the antennae of live (Venue:movable) or dead (Venue:immobile) bees were alternately brought into contact with a donor's head. Donors showed significantly higher rates of nectar offering toward live bees. These results indicate that mechanical stimulation generated by the recipients' antennal vibration contributes to initiating trophallaxis. Our findings suggest that honeybee antennae function not only as sensory organs but also as effectors that generate mechanical signals, providing new insights into social insect communication and informing bio-inspired systems such as swarm robotics.
